      What would you do first? 👇

      What if you were the last person on Earth? Follow the real science of what happens to New York, the power grid, zoo animals and landmarks like Mount Rushmore over 1,000 years.
      Honey found in Egyptian tombs was reportedly still edible, and the bootprints on the Moon could last millions of years.
      #whatif #lastpersononearth #science #curiosities #thecuriolab

      Por qué esta idea

      The hook states the topic as a question in under 2 seconds and plants a mystery (the blinking light) that viewers stay to resolve. Second-person narration makes the apocalypse personal, and each chapter escalates the scale from your phone to your city to all of human history. Verified, surprising facts (eternal honey, 13 million gallons, Rushmore erosion, Moon footprints) feel like rewards that drive rewatches and shares. US landmarks give American viewers instant recognition. The SOS twist flips 'last person' into 'not alone', and the closing line loops into the opening question, which boosts retention and replays. Comment bait: 'What would you do first?'

      Guion
      1. What if you were Earth's last person?
      2. You wake up tomorrow morning, and every human on Earth is gone.
      3. Stay until the end, because something out there is blinking in the dark.
      4. It's six a.m. in Manhattan, and your alarm never goes off.
      5. No horns, no sirens, no footsteps. Just wind between the buildings.
      6. You step outside, and Fifth Avenue is completely, impossibly, terrifyingly empty.
      7. Taxis and delivery trucks sit frozen in the street, doors hanging open.
      8. Coffee still steams on a counter, but nobody is there to drink it.
      9. You grab your phone and try to call anyone at all.
      10. No Service. And it's not a glitch. It's the beginning.
      11. Cell towers run on backup batteries that last only a few hours.
      12. Once those batteries die, every call, every text, every map dies with them.
      13. So you scream into the empty street, hoping anyone will answer.
      14. Your voice bounces off the glass towers, and nothing comes back.
      15. You sprint toward Times Square, searching for even a single face.
      16. The giant screens are still glowing, but they begin to flicker.
      17. By evening, the lights start failing across the city, block by block.
      18. Streetlights die one after another, like dominoes falling in slow motion.
      19. By midnight, Manhattan is pitch black from river to river.
      20. You sit down on the curb in the dark. Alone.
      21. That was only day one. And the next part is worse.
      22. One week later. The first thing you notice is that the hum is gone.
      23. Power plants need people to monitor them around the clock, every single day.
      24. With no operators, safety systems trip and plants shut down automatically.
      25. Plant after plant drops off the grid, until the whole network goes silent.
      26. Within days, the grid across America is completely dark.
      27. You grab a flashlight from a pharmacy and keep walking through the dark.
      28. Your heart pounds, because every shadow could suddenly be something alive.
      29. Meanwhile, across the country, zoos are sitting unwatched, with no keepers at all.
      30. Animals haven't been fed in days, and they are hungry and restless.
      31. Electric fences stop working, and locks without power stop holding.
      32. Enclosures fail. Gates swing open. Walls and cages begin to give.
      33. Clever animals like bears and monkeys find the weak spots first.
      34. Soon, a giraffe is strolling down an empty highway like she owns it.
      35. You blink twice. You rub your eyes. You are definitely not dreaming.
      36. She's taller than the streetlights, and she doesn't even glance at you.
      37. For the first time in a week, you actually laugh out loud.
      38. But the laughter fades. Predators don't need a cage to find dinner.
      39. You lock yourself inside a hardware store and barricade the door with shelves.
      40. You sleep with one eye open, listening to every creak.
      41. Then your phone's battery dies, the last glowing screen in the city.
      42. Now it's truly dark. Nothing electric works anywhere in the entire country.
      43. And soon you'll find out that food is your biggest problem.
      44. Day thirty. You have survived one whole month completely alone.
      45. You've raided every supermarket within a mile, filling backpacks with anything edible.
      46. But fresh food rots fast. Meat, milk, and fruit spoil within days.
      47. The smell is unbearable, so you stick to cans and boxes.
      48. Then, at the back of a dusty shelf, you spot a jar of honey.
      49. Honey basically never spoils, which makes it one of the best survival foods.
      50. Archaeologists found honey in ancient Egyptian tombs, thousands of years old, and still edible.
      51. So you eat a spoonful, and it tastes like sweet, golden survival.
      52. Water is the next problem, because the taps finally run dry.
      53. So you set buckets on the roof to collect rainwater.
      54. Every single drop counts, so you drink it slowly and carefully.
      55. You boil it first, because clean water is a matter of life and death.
      56. But you're not the only living thing getting desperate out here.
      57. At night, you notice pairs of eyes watching you from the darkness.
      58. Pet dogs, abandoned and starving, are turning into feral packs.
      59. Small breeds don't last long without humans to feed and protect them.
      60. But the big dogs survive, and they learn to hunt together.
      61. One pack spots you in the open, and you run for your life.
      62. Your lungs burn, your legs scream, and the barking gets closer.
      63. You dive through a doorway and slam it shut behind you.
      64. Claws scratch at the wood for hours, then finally fade away.
      65. And you realize that finding food was never the hardest part.
      66. Being alone, with no one to talk to, is much harder.
      67. So you start talking to yourself, just to hear a human voice.
      68. But all of that is nothing compared to what nature does next.
      69. One year in. Nature has been waiting for exactly this moment.
      70. Deep beneath your feet, the New York subway is a river waiting to happen.
      71. Normally, pumps remove about thirteen million gallons of water from it every day.
      72. Rain and underground springs keep seeping in, nonstop, day and night.
      73. But now the pumps have no power, and they're completely silent.
      74. Within days, some tunnels fill with water, and stations become lakes.
      75. Above ground, something green is slowly pushing its way through the pavement.
      76. Seeds settle into tiny cracks in the asphalt, waiting for rain.
      77. Grass first, then weeds, then tiny trees reaching for sunlight.
      78. Roots pry the concrete apart, one slow inch at a time.
      79. Within a few years, entire avenues start to look like meadows.
      80. Then one morning, you see something moving in Times Square.
      81. A deer. Then five. Then a whole herd of them.
      82. They graze right where tourists once stood shoulder to shoulder.
      83. For the first time in months, you smile, and you mean it.
      84. Birds nest inside billboards, and coyotes wander through empty parking lots.
      85. The planet isn't dying without us. It's finally breathing again.
      86. But the city you remember will not survive the coming winters.
      87. Water seeps into every crack, then freezes and expands with huge force.
      88. Out of habit, you still check your phone. Still nothing.
      89. So you stop counting days, and start counting years instead.
      90. And as the years pile up, the buildings begin to give up.
      91. Years ten through fifty. This is when the cities finally crumble.
      92. Take the bridges. Steel needs fresh paint to survive the weather.
      93. With no one painting it, rust eats the metal, season after season.
      94. Cables weaken, roadways sag, and the whole structure starts to groan.
      95. Then, one winter night, a bridge collapses into the river below.
      96. You hear it from miles away, a deep, groaning crash.
      97. Skyscrapers don't fall so fast, but they suffer too.
      98. Rain gets in, winter freezes it, and cracks widen every single year.
      99. Windows shatter and rain glass down onto the empty streets.
      100. Concrete crumbles, and steel beams twist and groan under their own weight.
      101. Then a thunderstorm rolls in, and lightning strikes a dry, rotting rooftop.
      102. Normally, firefighters would put out that spark in a few minutes.
      103. Now, nobody comes. The fire just keeps on spreading.
      104. Flames leap from building to building, block after block, all night.
      105. You watch the glow from a distant hilltop, powerless.
      106. It burns for days, until heavy rain finally puts it out.
      107. And when the ashes settle, nature moves in even faster.
      108. Forests swallow the ruins, and roots grow right through old living rooms.
      109. By now, you're old, gray-haired, but still walking every single day.
      110. Still alone. Still listening, every night, for another human voice.
      111. Sometimes you hear footsteps behind you. Then you realize it's only wind.
      112. Humanity's footprint is fading much faster than you ever imagined.
      113. But a few things refuse to disappear. Not even in a thousand years.
      114. Now fast forward one thousand years. Imagine you could watch from above.
      115. Forests cover every city, and rivers carve brand new paths to the sea.
      116. Almost everything we built has dissolved into dirt, rust, and roots.
      117. But some things are built to last. Like Mount Rushmore, in South Dakota.
      118. Its granite erodes only about one inch every ten thousand years.
      119. That means four presidents are still staring out over the Black Hills.
      120. Weeds grow at their feet, and nobody is there to see them.
      121. Across the ocean, the pyramids of Giza still stand in the desert.
      122. They are already more than four thousand years old.
      123. They've survived all that time, so another thousand is nothing for them.
      124. Now look up, past the clouds, at the Moon.
      125. In 1969, Neil Armstrong left bootprints in the lunar dust.
      126. The Moon has no wind and no rain to wipe them away.
      127. Only tiny meteorites and radiation wear them down, very, very slowly.
      128. Scientists say those footprints could last for millions of years.
      129. Long after our cities vanish, those prints will still be there.
      130. That's what's left of us: granite, stone, and a few footprints.
      131. But wait. Let's go back to the night you were alone.
      132. You climb to a rooftop in Manhattan, with the stars wide open above.
      133. Then something catches your eye, far away on the horizon.
      134. A light, tiny and far away, blinking in the dark.
      135. You freeze, and your heart starts hammering inside your chest.
      136. It blinks three short flashes, and then it pauses.
      137. Then three long flashes, slow and steady, almost like it's breathing.
      138. Then three short flashes again, exactly the same as before.
      139. That's SOS. The universal signal for someone who needs help.
      140. Someone else is out there. Someone alive. And they can see your city.
      141. You grab your flashlight with shaking hands and signal back.
      142. The light stops. For one long second, there is only silence.
      143. Then it blinks again. Three short. Three long. Three short.
      144. Maybe you were never really the last person on Earth.
      145. So… are you really the last person on Earth?
