      What If Every Human Disappeared? The Last Thing Left of Us

      largo-19-01-r1 · que_pasaria_si

      What would you leave behind for 10,000 years? 👇

      What happens if every human disappears from Earth? Follow the planet at 1 hour, 1 day, 1 year, 100 years and 10,000 years to find the last proof that we ever existed.
      The New York subway pumps out about 13 million gallons of groundwater every day, and without humans it starts to flood in about a day and a half. And Mount Rushmore's granite erodes only about 1 inch every 10,000 years.
      What would you leave behind for 10,000 years?
      #whatif #worldwithoutus #earth #curiosities

      Por qué esta idea

      It fulfills the exact request (cities, animals, power plants, buildings and nature at 1 hour, 1 day, 1 year, 100 years and 10,000 years) but packages it as a mystery with a question that only gets answered at the end, which drives up retention. It avoids the format he rejected ('Last Person on Earth'), because there are no survivors here, just a witness, and the star is the planet itself. It follows the pattern of the titles he approved ('What If You Dug Straight Down? Where You'd Really Come Out'): a 'What If' plus a concrete surprise. The data is well documented (Weisman, Chernobyl, the National Park Service on Rushmore's erosion), and the twist is iconic for a US audience. Suggested title: 'What If Every Human Disappeared? The Last Thing Left of Us #shorts'.

      Guion
      1. If humans vanished, what proves we existed?
      2. Imagine every human on Earth vanishes at once. No warning, no bodies, nobody left. And you're the only witness.
      3. We'll jump forward one hour, one day, one year, a hundred years, and ten thousand. Every jump knocks out a suspect.
      4. Stay to the end, because the one thing still standing is something you've seen a thousand times, and it's not what you think.
      5. Right now it's noon in Times Square. Taxis sit with their doors wide open, and a coffee still steams on a table.
      6. A dog trots into the empty street, ears up, looking for anyone. The crosswalk signal keeps counting for nobody.
      7. Out on the highway, cars drift to a stop. No hands on the wheel, no foot on the gas, no one anywhere.
      8. Inside a power plant, the control room sits silent. Screens glow, alarms blink, and nobody is there to answer them.
      9. One hour later. The first suspect for last proof is the city lights. Let's see how long they last.
      10. Power plants depend on constant human attention: fuel deliveries, maintenance, repairs. Without crews, the grid starts to stumble.
      11. A transformer overloads and bursts in a shower of sparks. Then another. Whole neighborhoods go dark in a ripple.
      12. At a nuclear plant, sensors notice the trouble and drop the control rods automatically. The reactor shuts itself down. It's called a SCRAM.
      13. Good news. But the spent fuel stored in cooling pools still needs pumps, and pumps need power.
      14. Backup diesel generators kick in. They hold for days, maybe a couple of weeks, but nobody is coming to refuel them.
      15. Traffic lights flicker and die, one block after another, until the whole city is lit only by the sun.
      16. Suspect number one, the electric glow of civilization, is already crossed out. Next stop, one day later.
      17. Twenty-four hours in. Beneath New York, there's a hidden crew that never sleeps, and it's made of pumps.
      18. The subway sits below the water table. Every single day, pumps remove about thirteen million gallons of groundwater.
      19. That's almost twenty Olympic swimming pools, every single day, just to keep the trains running dry.
      20. Now the pumps are off. Water seeps up through the tunnel walls, trickling across the rails.
      21. According to author Alan Weisman in The World Without Us, some stations could begin flooding within about thirty-six hours.
      22. Down on the platform, an empty train waits with its doors open as the water climbs past the tracks.
      23. And below the city, ants and other insects pour out of the tunnels, fleeing the rising water.
      24. Above ground, entire cities are in blackout. Refrigerators warm up, and food quietly starts to spoil in every kitchen.
      25. Supermarkets, hospitals, offices, all dark. A phone buzzes in an abandoned apartment, then its screen goes black.
      26. Outside, pets are confused. Dogs wait by doors. Cats stare out windows, wondering where dinner went.
      27. Around the world, the same thing happens in mines, tunnels, and basements, all slowly filling with water.
      28. Suspect number two, the great underground network, is nothing but a river in the dark. Crossed out. Now, a full year.
      29. One year later. Nature doesn't wait. It starts moving back in, and we have a real-world preview of how.
      30. In 1986, the Chernobyl disaster forced everyone out of a huge zone in Ukraine. People left, and the wild came back.
      31. Today, wolves, wild boar, deer, and even wild horses roam the abandoned towns of the exclusion zone.
      32. Picture it: a wolf pack slips between Soviet-era apartment blocks while horses graze where a parking lot used to be.
      33. Back in our empty world, house cats and dogs go feral. Small breeds struggle, but big dogs form packs.
      34. Meanwhile, farm animals break through broken fences and wander into open fields, free for the first time.
      35. In the cities, weeds push through cracks in the sidewalk. A crack becomes a gap. A gap becomes a garden.
      36. Lawns turn into meadows. Without mowing, grass grows waist high, and seeds ride the wind into every empty lot.
      37. Rain washes dirt into the gutters, and seeds find it. By next spring, storm drains are clogged and streets turn into shallow streams.
      38. Insects, which were here long before us, swarm into every open window. Spiders spin webs across storefronts.
      39. Rain pours through broken windows into offices. Paper swells, carpets rot, and mold starts blooming across the walls.
      40. The cars on the freeway? They're still there. But vines are already climbing the bumpers.
      41. Without pumps, the water in those nuclear cooling pools slowly heats up and starts to evaporate.
      42. Scientists warn that some of those sites could cause local damage. Nature would have to handle the rest.
      43. Even so, in places like Chernobyl, life comes back faster than anyone expected. Wildlife isn't waiting for permission.
      44. One year in, the world has already changed. But nothing we built has truly fallen yet. Give it a century.
      45. One hundred years later. Everything made of steel faces its worst enemy: water and air.
      46. Steel bridges need fresh paint constantly. Without it, rust eats through the metal, bolt by bolt.
      47. Then one winter, a rusted bridge groans, sags, and finally snaps. Tons of metal plunge into the river.
      48. Many other bridges follow, one by one. Rivers that were once tamed return to their old paths.
      49. Wooden houses go next. Rain soaks the beams, termites move in, and roofs cave into living rooms.
      50. Whole suburbs collapse into piles of timber, and ferns grow out of old couches.
      51. Trees burst through asphalt. Roots lift entire roads like slabs of chocolate.
      52. Forests swallow the suburbs. Deer sleep in backyards, and foxes raise their pups in garages.
      53. Rivers flood old highways. Beavers return and build dams in the drainage ditches.
      54. Concrete, our favorite material, cracks as water seeps in and freezes, again and again, every winter.
      55. Skyscrapers are tougher, but wind, rain, and freezing winters crack their windows. Plants take root on their ledges.
      56. Wild animals take over the high-rises: peregrine falcons nest on old towers, and raccoons own the lobbies.
      57. Meanwhile, deep in the oceans, whales can finally hear each other again. Ship noise is gone.
      58. At night, the sky looks like something we haven't seen in generations: no light pollution, just millions of stars.
      59. Suspect number three, our bridges, our houses, our suburbs, all crossed out. But the biggest structures are still holding on.
      60. Plastic is still out there too, broken into tiny pieces, drifting in rivers and soil for centuries.
      61. So the question gets harder. Is there anything built to last longer than all this?
      62. Ten thousand years later. Longer than all of recorded history. Writing was invented only about five thousand years ago.
      63. The cities are green mounds. Rain, roots, and ice have ground glass, steel, and concrete back into soil.
      64. Rivers have changed course, burying old foundations under mud, sand, and stones.
      65. Even the Great Pyramid, now about forty-five hundred years old, would look very different from today.
      66. Glass, plastic, and nuclear waste remain, buried deep in the soil. Traces, but nothing anyone would call a monument.
      67. Far beyond Earth, the Voyager probes keep drifting, carrying a golden record. But they're out in space, not here.
      68. Where Central Park used to be, a forest older than any we've known grows. Nobody remembers it was ever a park.
      69. An explorer from another world would walk through endless wild forest, finding almost nothing that says we were here.
      70. Then, in the middle of the Black Hills of South Dakota, something catches the light.
      71. Four enormous faces, sixty feet tall, are still staring out at the horizon.
      72. Mount Rushmore. Carved between 1927 and 1941 into solid granite, one of the most durable rocks on Earth.
      73. The National Park Service estimates the granite there erodes only about one inch every ten thousand years.
      74. One inch. After ten thousand years of wind, rain, and ice, those faces have lost barely a fingertip's width.
      75. So Washington, Jefferson, Roosevelt, and Lincoln are still there, waiting for someone to ask who they were.
      76. Our skyscrapers, our subways, our bridges, even our power plants, all gone. But four stone faces remain.
      77. And here's the irony: nature, which erased everything else, ended up leaving us the one thing we carved into it.
      78. That's the answer to our mystery. The last thing proving we were ever here is a mountain that looks like us.
      79. Now it's your turn. If you could leave one thing behind to last ten thousand years, what would it be?
      80. Tell us in the comments. And if you liked this one, watch our next video: what if you dug straight down?
