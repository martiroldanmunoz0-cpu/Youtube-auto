      What If You Landed on Every Planet Without a Spacesuit?

      largo-11-01-r1 · que_pasaria_si

      ✅ Revisión automática: todo correcto

      Would you go to Titan just to fly? 👇

      What would happen if you landed on every planet without a spacesuit? We rank the Sun, Jupiter, Venus, Mercury, the Moon, Mars and Titan from deadliest to most survivable.
      Including the true story of NASA technician Jim LeBlanc, who was exposed to near vacuum in 1965 and survived.
      #whatif #solarsystem #space #nasa #science

      Por qué esta idea

      Combina los tres formatos que el dueño ha aprobado: el 'qué pasaría si' inmersivo, el mito contra realidad ('¿explotas en el espacio?') y una historia real con protagonista con nombre, como Yamaguchi. Además no repite ninguno de los temas ya hechos. El formato ranking hace que cada capítulo sea un mini-gancho ('¿cuál será el número 1?') y sostiene la retención durante 8-10 minutos. La historia de LeBlanc reengancha en la mitad, justo cuando suele caer la audiencia. El giro de Titán es sorprendente, verificable y muy comentable ('I'd go to Titan just to fly'). Todos los datos están bien documentados por la NASA.

      Guion
      1. How long would you survive on every planet?
      2. Today we teleport you to every world in the Solar System, no spacesuit, and rank them from deadliest to most survivable.
      3. On one of them, you'd last less than a second. And the number one spot is not where you think it is.
      4. Stay until the end, because there's a place where you'd only need a mask and a warm coat. And it gets stranger.
      5. Number seven: the Sun. You appear right at its surface, where the temperature is about ten thousand degrees Fahrenheit.
      6. That's about five times hotter than lava pouring out of a volcano. Your body doesn't burn. It simply stops being a body.
      7. Within a fraction of a second, you're vaporized, turned into a puff of hot gas before you can even blink or feel pain.
      8. And the heat isn't even the only problem. The Sun's gravity at its surface is about twenty-eight times stronger than Earth's.
      9. Good news: you didn't feel a thing. Bad news: we have six more worlds to go, and they're not much kinder.
      10. Number six: Jupiter. The biggest planet in the Solar System. More than one thousand Earths could fit inside it.
      11. But Jupiter has no ground. It's a giant ball of gas and liquid, so there's nothing to stand on. You just fall.
      12. You tumble through freezing clouds, caught in winds that blow at more than four hundred miles per hour.
      13. That's faster than any hurricane on Earth. And the Great Red Spot, a storm about as wide as our whole planet, has raged for centuries.
      14. Meanwhile, Jupiter's gravity pulls you two and a half times harder than Earth's. A one hundred eighty pound person would weigh four hundred fifty.
      15. Dizzy, heavy, and falling deeper, you're now in the dark. The pressure climbs with every mile, and the air around you keeps heating up.
      16. Deep down, the pressure becomes thousands of times greater than at sea level, then millions. You never hit bottom. There is no bottom.
      17. Long before that, the pressure and heat end it. Number six: gone within minutes, falling through a sky with no floor.
      18. Number five: Venus. Earth's evil twin. It's almost the same size as our planet, so you'd think it would be friendly.
      19. It isn't. The surface sits at about nine hundred degrees Fahrenheit, hot enough to melt lead like butter in a skillet.
      20. And the air presses on you ninety times harder than on Earth. That's like diving nearly three thousand feet under the ocean.
      21. Picture a submarine at that depth. Now picture yourself without one. The pressure squeezes from every side while you cook at the same time.
      22. Above you, thick yellow clouds of sulfuric acid drift by. Not exactly the place for a relaxing walk or a deep breath.
      23. Here's the weird part: Venus is hotter than Mercury, even though it's almost twice as far from the Sun. The reason is a runaway greenhouse effect.
      24. Its thick carbon dioxide air traps heat like a blanket. You'd last a few seconds before the heat and pressure finish the job.
      25. Even the best Soviet Venera probe, built like a tank, lasted only about two hours on the surface before it died.
      26. You, meanwhile, have no tank. So let's leave this oven behind and head to the planet closest to the Sun.
      27. Number four: Mercury. The smallest planet, and the closest to the Sun. You'd think it would be the hottest. Well, not exactly.
      28. On the sunny side, it's about eight hundred degrees Fahrenheit. That's as hot as a pizza oven, and you're standing right inside it.
      29. Your skin starts to burn in seconds. And with almost no air, there's nothing to breathe and nothing to carry the heat away.
      30. But here's the twist. Mercury barely has an atmosphere, so all that heat escapes into space the moment the Sun goes down.
      31. At night, it plunges to about minus two hundred ninety degrees Fahrenheit. That's a swing of more than one thousand degrees in a single day.
      32. Frozen solid, in a vacuum, on a world where one full day lasts about one hundred seventy-six Earth days.
      33. Either way, you're done within seconds. Mercury burns you or freezes you, depending on where you land. Now, the Moon.
      34. Number three: the Moon. And here we have to bust a myth you've seen in a hundred movies.
      35. In the movies, someone gets pushed out of an airlock and explodes like a balloon. Dramatic, right? It's also completely wrong.
      36. So what really happens? The first rule: don't hold your breath. The air in your lungs would expand and tear them, so you must exhale.
      37. Next, the moisture on your tongue starts to bubble. Not from heat, but because without pressure, liquids boil at low temperatures.
      38. Your body swells as water vapor forms inside your tissues. You could grow to about twice your size, but your skin is tough enough to hold.
      39. No explosion. Your skin is strong and stretchy. But the oxygen in your blood runs out, and you have about fifteen seconds of consciousness.
      40. Then you black out. Your blood doesn't boil either, because your blood vessels hold the pressure. But your brain is starved of oxygen.
      41. Death comes in about one or two minutes. And the Moon adds insult to injury: roughly 250 degrees in sunlight, around minus 200 at night.
      42. But wait. Has anyone really tested this on a human? Yes. One man actually lived through it, and his story is wild. Stay with me.
      43. Houston, Texas, 1965. The space race is in full swing, and NASA is testing spacesuits for astronauts heading to space.
      44. Meet Jim LeBlanc, a NASA technician. His job: wear a spacesuit inside a giant vacuum chamber to make sure it works.
      45. The chamber pumped out the air to copy conditions at around one hundred twenty thousand feet, where the air is far too thin to breathe.
      46. Then, in the middle of the test, a hose came loose from his suit. The air began rushing out, and the suit started to lose pressure fast.
      47. He knew right away that something was terribly wrong. The suit went slack, and around him, the chamber was nearly airless.
      48. His last memory was a strange feeling: the saliva on his tongue starting to bubble, and then to boil. Then everything faded.
      49. About fourteen seconds later, he lost consciousness. The same clock you'd have on the Moon. Same science, but now it was real.
      50. Outside the chamber, his coworkers saw it all happen. Without hesitating, they started letting air back in. Every second counted.
      51. In under half a minute, the air was back. As the pressure rose, Jim woke up, and he walked away with no lasting damage.
      52. The accident gave scientists rare real data: a person can survive a brief exposure to near vacuum, as long as someone rescues them fast.
      53. So back to our Moon scenario: you wouldn't explode, but you'd have less than half a minute to be saved. And nobody's coming. Next: Mars.
      54. Number two: Mars. The planet everyone wants to colonize, and the one that looks most like Earth. Surely we can survive there?
      55. Not even close. The air pressure on Mars is less than one percent of Earth's. That's like standing about twenty miles above our planet.
      56. That's far below the Armstrong limit, where your saliva boils at body temperature. Just like on the Moon, you swell up and pass out.
      57. The average temperature is about minus eighty degrees Fahrenheit, as cold as the coldest day ever recorded in Alaska.
      58. And the thin air is about ninety-five percent carbon dioxide. So even if you could hold your breath, there's no oxygen to breathe.
      59. Dust storms can swallow the entire planet and last for weeks, blocking the sunlight. And the radiation is far stronger than on Earth.
      60. On Mars, you'd last about the same as on the Moon, a couple of minutes at most. Less deadly than Venus, but only just.
      61. So Mars, Earth's twin? Not really. But there's one world where the air is thick, the pressure is friendly, and you'd survive. Any guesses?
      62. Number one is not Mars, not the Moon, and not even a planet. It's Titan, Saturn's biggest moon, more than seven hundred million miles away.
      63. Titan is bigger than the planet Mercury. And it's the only moon in the Solar System with a thick atmosphere, mostly nitrogen, just like ours.
      64. The air pressure on its surface is about one and a half times Earth's. That's like diving about fifteen feet down in a swimming pool.
      65. That means no boiling saliva, no swelling body, and no need for a pressure suit. The thick air pushes back and keeps you together.
      66. But don't take off your coat. It's about minus two hundred ninety degrees Fahrenheit, and the air has no oxygen, so you'd need a mask.
      67. Overhead, an orange sky. Around you, rivers and lakes of liquid methane and ethane. Real rain, but made of fuel instead of water.
      68. So all you really need is an insanely warm coat and an oxygen mask. Nothing else on Titan would hurt you right away.
      69. And now the best part. Titan's gravity is only about one seventh of Earth's. You'd feel feather-light, and every step would turn into a leap.
      70. Thick air plus low gravity makes something amazing possible. Scientists say a human with wings strapped to their arms could actually fly by flapping.
      71. So yes, the most survivable place in the entire Solar System is also the only place where you could fly like a bird.
      72. That's the ranking: from vaporized in a blink to flapping your arms above a methane lake. Not a bad trip, right?
      73. Now tell me: would you go to Titan just to fly, or is Mars still your pick? Let me know in the comments.
      74. If you liked this, watch our next video, where we find out what would happen if you fell into a black hole. See you there.
